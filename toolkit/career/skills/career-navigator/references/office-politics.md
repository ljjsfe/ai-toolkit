# Office Politics — power, interests, information

Definition: where there are people, there is politics. People will use authority / power to push their own agenda — that's the norm.
This module's stance: **politics isn't "whether to participate," it's "whether you can read it."** Someone who can't read it defaults to being a pawn on someone else's board. The goal is to understand the structure, protect yourself, and win the allocation of scope, headcount and recognition. Leverage is part of the job: interests, headcount, narrative and timing are the real currency. What this skill excludes is lying — invented offers, false facts, rumors — not because it is impolite but because it gets found out, and credibility is the asset every other move depends on.

## Step one is always: draw the power map

Before answering any politics question, have the user fill in (or infer from the conversation):

```
Who makes the final call? (formal authority)
Who can influence that person? (informal power: seniority, personal ties to upper management, control of a key system/client)
Who's competing for what resource? (headcount, budget, project ownership, voice, the next promotion slot)
Where am I on this map? Who needs me? Who do I need?
Any recent org changes / new leader / re-org / strategy shift? (power reshuffles are the most dangerous AND the most opportune periods)
```

Without this map, every piece of advice is a guess.

## Five types of politics + the corresponding play

| Type | What it looks like | Play |
|---|---|---|
| **Resource competition** | Fighting over projects, people, scope | Let your manager be the gatekeeper; don't be the bad guy yourself; use written scope definitions to prevent drift |
| **Information politics** | You're the last to know; excluded from meetings; information asymmetry | Build horizontal information channels (peer 1:1s, other team leaders); proactively send updates to own the narrative |
| **Credit/blame** | Credit-stealing, blame-shifting | Narrate as you go + written milestone updates; clarify negative attributions only verbally |
| **Taking sides** | Two leaders don't get along; you're asked to pick a side | Don't take sides; with both sides, talk only about the work and the facts; never carry messages |
| **Power vacuum/reshuffle** | Re-org, new manager, strategy shift | The first 30 days are critical: build a direct relationship with the new leader, reconfirm priorities |

## How power is actually allocated (big-company mechanics)

Figures below are one former Amazon VP's observations; your company's differ — ask, don't assume.

- **Headcount is the currency.** Impact is hard to measure and easy to argue; headcount is
  neither. Manager and director promotions often carry a size bar, written or not (he cites
  roughly 80–90 people for Director in some orgs). So any proposal that moves people into or
  out of an org is also a negotiation about someone's promotion. Ask whose.
- **Shell games.** Executives lend a team to a favoured leader for a few months to clear a size
  bar, then move it back after the promotion. A team transfer near promo season is a signal.
- **Reorgs have two reasons.** The stated one: a new market, an acquisition, a leader leaving.
  The real ones, often: keep a star by handing them attractive scope, build someone's promotion
  case, or push someone out. Executives are storytellers — the reorg is a narrative built to
  justify the allocation upward. When one is announced, map who gains scope or headcount, who
  loses it, and whose case it builds.
- **The garbage can.** Moving someone into legacy maintenance, a support queue or "other" is how
  companies push out people they don't want to fire. If it happens to you, it is red — see
  `exits-and-severance.md`.
- **Lost scope rarely means demotion.** A director whose org shrinks usually gets 6–9 months to
  find matching scope. The clock starts at the reorg, not when someone mentions it.
- **Never hand back headcount.** When a project dies, move its people to a winning project before
  leadership notices — once they have started, the ship has sailed. Returned headcount rarely
  comes back. Expect the opposite move from above: senior leaders cut inflated asks line by line
  (Bezos-style, down to two or three people per project), so every head you hold needs a story.
- **A manager can push out almost any IC.** A manager in good standing has dozens of ways to make
  someone leave, and HR's job is to protect the company, which in practice means the manager —
  unless the manager has a legal problem (retaliation, discrimination). Plan as if that is true.
- **Framing goes to whoever arrives first.** In a dispute, the first person to give the skip-level
  or HR their version sets the frame everyone else is measured against — more than half the
  battle. If a conflict is coming, get your facts in first, spoken, calm, delivery-focused.

## Getting inside the circle

Insiders are the people decision-makers already trust and consult before deciding. You get there
by being useful to power, early and repeatedly — not by being liked.

- **Pre-wire every decision.** Before the meeting where something is decided, talk one-on-one
  with each stakeholder: "Before Thursday, I wanted to get your read on this." People who were
  consulted feel respected and arrive as allies; people surprised in the room arrive as critics.
  Executives are anxious about anything that could make them look bad, and the person who
  removes surprises becomes the one they trust when it matters.
- **Solve the power holder's problem**, not just yours — see `managing-up.md`. Usefulness to the
  person with power is the entry ticket.
- **Show up in the crisis.** Trust at senior levels is built in outages and escalations
  (`review-and-promotion.md`). The inner circle is mostly people who were there when it broke.
- **Use chaos.** Order protects incumbents; a reorg, a leader leaving or a new AI mandate resets
  the board. In the first weeks, volunteer for the orphaned scope and the problem nobody owns —
  that is when the new leader decides who they can count on.
- **Attach early to a rising leader.** Backing someone before they are powerful costs little and
  is remembered; backing them after costs the same as everyone else's flattery.
- **Keep a favour ledger.** Note what you have done for each ally and what they have done for you
  (the "What I've helped them with" column of the sponsor map). Allies are kept by reciprocity,
  not by affection.

## Turf: defending and taking scope

**When another org wants to absorb yours:**
- **Skunk.** Put the full cost of what they would inherit on the table in the forum where it is
  decided: legacy systems, on-call load, ticket volume, open incidents. "Here are the ten
  systems that come with this, and the on-call they need — are you ready to staff that?"
- **Porcupine.** Make absorbing you expensive: counter-propose the merge in the other direction,
  with a cost story ("combining them under us saves 30%"). Only with your skip-level behind you;
  without that it reads as insubordination.

**When you want to take scope:**
- Never "they're failing, give it to me." Go to the shared boss or their lead with an offer of
  help on a visible problem: "I noticed X has been having a rough time — is there something my
  team can do?" Deliver. Then propose consolidating, with numbers: cost saved, fewer handoffs,
  faster releases.
- Sell the bigger value story (double the release rate, double the revenue), not the other
  team's weakness.

**When a counterpart is toxic:** assign one resilient person as the single interface, tell them
plainly that shielding the team is the job, and reward them visibly afterwards.

## Escalating over a bad manager: never alone

Going to the skip-level alone makes you the high-maintenance one: replacing a manager is
expensive for the skip, so their first instinct is to doubt the messenger.

1. Privately, spoken, find two or three peers with the same problem: "Have you been running into
   the same thing with X?"
2. Go together, or one after another: "Three of us have hit the same issue. If it helps, we can
   each walk you through specifics."
3. Facts and delivery impact only — no motives, no adjectives. Several voices with the same facts
   make it the manager's problem instead of yours.

## Threat grading (not all politics deserves your energy)

- **Red**: directly affects your rating / promotion / project ownership / job security → must act, and should bring in your manager or sponsor
- **Yellow**: affects visibility or relationships, but doesn't directly affect your evaluation → watch + low-cost prevention (send more updates, schedule more 1:1s)
- **Green**: a matter between other people → don't get involved, don't comment, don't carry messages

The vast majority of "office politics" is yellow and green. The most common mistake, for engineers whose home workplace culture differs from US norms (for example, many immigrant engineers), is hitting one of two extremes: treating green as red (anxiety, overreaction), or treating red as green ("I'll just do good work and that's enough").

## Hard rules for self-protection

1. **Keep it professional, don't overshare** — personal life, complaints about the company or people, salary, thoughts about leaving, visa anxiety — none of it gets said. Any overshare can be used against you.
2. **Positive stays written, negative only spoken** (SKILL.md principle 7).
3. **Don't take sides, don't carry messages, don't evaluate a third party.** If someone comes to you to complain, respond: "Have you shared that with them directly?"
4. **Don't assume hostility.** Ask about intent first. Most "politics" is someone else solving their own problem, not targeting you. But —
5. **Assume good faith once, verify once; a second occurrence is a pattern.** If the same person does the same thing a second time, stop giving the benefit of the doubt — start keeping a record (for yourself, don't send it: date, what was said, who was there) and make sure your manager knows the facts.
6. **Don't isolate yourself.** People with no horizontal relationships are the first to be sacrificed during a reshuffle, because no one speaks up for them.

## Offensive moves (the professional, legitimate kind)

- **Own the narrative**: send the scope doc first at project kickoff; send the update first at every milestone. Whoever writes it down first defines the facts.
- **Build a coalition**: cross-team collaboration, helping others create visibility, writing high-quality peer feedback — these are the people who speak for you in rooms you're not in.
- **Help your manager win**: know their deliverables and obstacles, and help solve them — their willingness to sponsor you depends on how useful you are to them.
- **Speak up with substance**: if you have a point of view in a meeting, say it. The silent are blank spots on the power map.
- **Push back with reason**: people who get pushed around are people who don't push back. One professional pushback defines your boundaries in other people's eyes.
- **Give credit**: publicly praise whoever deserves it (not sucking up). This is the cheapest relationship investment there is.
- **Give, then take**: help first, ask later.

## Warning signs: when the environment is no longer worth staying in

- Your manager changed, the new manager brought their own people, and hasn't proactively built a relationship with you within 30 days
- Your project has been broken up, merged, or "paused," with no equivalent new scope
- Two review cycles in a row with no feedback, or feedback that's vague
- Your sponsor has left / lost influence
- You're excluded from meetings that decide your work, with no explanation
- A reorg moves you into a role with no roadmap or growth path (the garbage can) → `exits-and-severance.md`

Two or more of these → this isn't a "work harder" problem — switch to `liu-ayi` for a structured analysis (stay / switch teams / switch companies), and start building external optionality immediately.

## Quick answers to common questions

**"Should I get involved in office politics?"** You're already in it. The question is whether you read it actively or get hit passively.
**"I don't want to become that kind of person."** Everything above is reading interests and using real leverage: headcount, narrative, timing, coalitions, the cost of saying no to you. Rumors and invented facts are not in it — they get found out and cost more than they win. If you still want out of the game, choose where you play: see "Choosing where to work" in `interview-and-resume.md`.
**"My manager plays favorites with someone."** First look at what that person is doing right (most likely alignment + visibility), and copy it — don't complain.
**"The new leader brought in their own people."** Set up a 1:1 within 30 days, ask about their priorities and "what does great look like," then prove yourself with results. You don't need to become one of their people — you need to become someone they can't afford to lose.
**"Should I tell my manager that so-and-so is scheming?"** Only state the facts and the impact on delivery — no motives, no adjectives, and say it out loud, not in writing.
