# Exits, PIPs & Severance — when the company is moving you out

Read the situation by what the company has already decided, not by what it says. The labels
("performance", "restructuring", "a new opportunity") are the narrative; the decision is usually
made before you hear it.

## Signs you are being moved out

- A formal PIP (performance improvement plan)
- A reorg puts you in the "other" bucket: legacy maintenance, a support queue, a team with no
  roadmap and no growth path — a role nobody asked for and nobody leaves upward from
- Your scope is split up with no equivalent new scope (also in office-politics warning signs)
- A sudden run of negative, documented feedback after a quiet period

Any one of these is red on the threat grading. Start the external search the same week; it is
far cheaper to leave with an offer in hand than with a deadline.

## What a PIP actually means

By the time a formal PIP is written, management has usually tried what it was going to try and
decided you should go. Recovery happens, but rarely. Spend the PIP period on two things:
finding the next job, and negotiating the exit. Do the PIP work well enough that it stays
"performance", not "conduct" — that keeps your reference and your story clean.

At senior levels, firings labelled "performance" are often a style or values mismatch with the
leader. Arguing the performance facts rarely changes a decision that was never about them.

## Your leverage in an exit

You hold more than it feels like:

1. **Time and process.** Every month of a contested PIP costs your manager hours of
   documentation and review. A quick, clean exit is worth money to them.
2. **The story.** What you say afterwards — to the team that stays, to people at the next
   company — is how this manager and team will be described. A graceful exit protects them.
3. **Their self-image.** Most managers want to see themselves as fair. Give them a way to be
   the good person in the story.
4. **Your signature.** Severance is usually paid in exchange for signing a release. That
   signature is what they are buying, so do not sign on the spot — ask for time to review it.

## Scripts

Asking for a better package (warm, firm, no threat):
> "This is a hard moment for me. I have a lot of friends on this team, and I'd really like this
> to be a graceful exit rather than something that leaves people feeling it was unfair. To give
> me enough runway to land the next role, could you help me get more on the financial side?
> I'd like to be able to speak well of you and the team when I leave."

When a negative conversation is sprung on you:
> "I want to give this the attention it deserves, and I don't think I can do that well right
> now. Can we pick it up first thing tomorrow?"

Most managers back off. Use the night to decide your ask, not to draft a rebuttal. If they press on
after you asked for time, note the date and their words in your private record — it is a card for
later, as long as you report it exactly as it happened.

When handed a severance agreement:
> "Thanks. I'd like to take a few days to review this properly before I sign."

## Don'ts

- Don't resign before you have negotiated; resigning gives away all four points of leverage.
- Don't fight the PIP in writing; negative stays spoken (SKILL.md principle 7).
- Don't trash the manager on the way out; the story is leverage only while you control it.
