# Tough Conversations, Push-back & Estimation — a script library

Principles: **with respect (privately), with reason (reasoning + alternative), feeling not judgment, nothing written or said when you're not calm.**

Before giving the user a script, ask yourself two things: (1) what is the other person's intent? (2) what decision should this message get them to make?

## State feelings, not judgments

- ❌ "You keep ignoring my input."
- ✅ "When the design changed without a heads-up, it made me feel my input wasn't considered. Going forward, can we loop me in before final decisions?"

Structure: **When [specific behavior], it makes me feel [feeling]. What I'd like is [specific request].**

## Push back on unreasonable requests

```
1. Acknowledge   "I understand why this matters for [their goal]."
2. Reason        "Here's my concern: [specific, ideally with a number/risk]."
3. Alternative   "What I can do is A by [date], or B if we drop C."
4. Ask           "Which would you prefer?"
```

Example:
"I get that the client wants this by Friday. My concern is that skipping the eval step means we'd ship without knowing the regression risk — last time that cost us two weeks. I can either ship a scoped-down version Friday with eval, or the full version next Wednesday. Which works better for you?"

**Stand firm when you have good reasons**: if the other person insists, repeat the reasoning once more + "I'll do it your way, but I want to flag the risk in writing so we're aligned" — this is disagree and commit, while also leaving a positive, professional record.

## Two-track estimation

> "If everything goes well, I can finish it in **X** days. If something unexpected happens — [name 1-2 specific things] — I'll need **Y** days."

Purpose: shows you're aggressive (X), while also setting the right expectation (Y) and giving yourself a buffer and a fallback.
**Estimation must come with reasoning**: why that much effort is needed. Break it into 1) known work 2) unknowns/dependencies 3) verification/eval time.

Never give a single number; never say "should be quick."

## Asked to commit to a date

The most common conflict between teams is a silent expectation gap: there is no contract, you
think March is a fine delivery, they needed January and March is too much, too late.

1. **Ask before you answer** a high-stakes "can you?". A few yes/no questions get them to lay
   out the timeline and what depends on it:
   > "Is January a drop-dead date, or a target? What happens downstream if it lands in March?"
2. **Answer factually, separating possible from commit:**
   > "January is possible, but my commit is March. I can give you a firmer answer in two weeks,
   > if that works for you."
3. **Anything short of no is heard as yes.** "There's a chance" is a yes to them; walking it back
   later is a decommit, and costs more than the original no. Don't soften the key point.
4. **Unpack the ask** into smaller decisions you can commit to separately. If they don't need a
   commit today, don't give one.

## Saying "no" / not having bandwidth

"I want to help with this. Right now I'm committed to X and Y through [date]. I can take it on after that, or if it's more urgent than X, I'm happy to check with [manager] on reprioritizing."
→ Hand the priority decision to your manager so you don't have to be the bad guy.

## Defending against the junk work

Whoever accepts junk work without friction gets all of it. Push back enough that handing it to
you costs more than handing it to someone softer (porcupine, not bunny).

- **Tokens, with a smile:** "I only get three junk-work tokens a month. If this has to use one, I
  can do it — but we'd be down to two. Sure you want to spend it here?" Your manager never agreed
  to tokens; the joke sets the frame that the supply is finite, without a fight.
- **Trade it:** "I know there's a pile of work nobody wants. I'll spend the next two weeks clearing
  those bugs and the ops backlog for you — and once that's done, I lead the next new project."
  Get the second half agreed before you start the first.

## Opening a hard conversation

- Schedule 15-30 minutes ahead of time, 1:1, spoken (not email)
- Opener: "I'd like to talk through something that's been on my mind — it's about X. I want to make sure we're on the same page."
- Discuss only one issue
- End with one concrete next step + a timeline

## Asking for a promotion / an opportunity

- "I'd like to be considered for [next level] in the next cycle. Based on the ladder, what do you see as the gaps, and what projects would demonstrate that scope?"
- "I heard the Y project is kicking off. I'd like to lead the Z part — I think it's a good fit because [reason], and it would give me the cross-functional exposure we talked about."

- When you have the idea, claim it in so many words: "I've found something that could change how
  our team does X. I'm really excited about it, and I want to be the one to build it." Once you've
  said it, giving it to someone else means your manager has to disappoint you face to face — most
  won't.

Never use "if possible," "maybe," "I was wondering if."

## When the carrot keeps dangling

Vague promises ("keep doing what you're doing") cost your manager nothing; make them specific.

> "I understand you can't guarantee the outcome — it goes through committee. I'm not asking for a
> guarantee. What do I need to deliver so that you can walk into that room 100% confident
> arguing for me?"

> "You've asked me to keep going, but I don't have a clear target or timeline. How can I keep
> working at full speed without knowing what I'm aiming for?"

"How" questions (Chris Voss) make the other side solve your problem instead of saying no to it.

**Life stage, said plainly** (only if it is true):
> "My career matters a lot to me. I'm in a window where I need to make the next step in the next
> two years — [the real reason: starting a family, supporting my parents]. I'd much rather do it
> here. But if there's no path here, I'll have to look outside."

Then stop talking. Hold eye contact and let the silence do the work; the first one to fill it
usually concedes something.

## When you're shot down / criticized

- Don't defend yourself right away: "Thanks for the feedback. Can you give me a specific example so I make sure I understand?"
- Separate fact from interpretation
- Respond 24 hours later if needed

## Receiving a negative email

Don't reply by email. Reply: "Thanks — let's grab 15 min to talk through this." Then resolve it verbally.

## Polite fictions — leverage without a threat

Say true things the other person cannot argue with, which carry your real position. They hear
the leverage; there is nothing in it to accuse you of. Warm in tone, firm in substance. Never
bluff: every one of these works only if what you imply is true and you would act on it.

**Leverage inventory — take it before playing any card:**

| | ask yourself | if weak |
|---|---|---|
| outside option | a live offer, or recruiters you could convert within weeks? | interview first; the card is empty until then |
| replaceability | how long and how much would replacing you cost them? | own something critical before you push |
| sponsor | who above your manager would argue for you? | seed one (review-and-promotion) |
| timing | where are you in the cycle — before calibration, mid-project, after a win? | wait for the window: right after a win, before calibration |
| record | is your recent delivery visible and undisputed? | fix visibility first (branding) |

**Card budget.** "I need to think about what that means" and a competing offer are one-shot: after
you play them, your manager treats you as a flight risk — expect them to either deliver or start
planning around you. Play one per cycle at most, and only when you would act on it. Porcupine
pushes and token jokes are repeatable but wear thin; spend them on work that matters.

**A promotion that keeps slipping:**
> "I need to understand how important my career growth is to the company. It's very important to
> me. If it isn't important to the company, I need to think about what that means."

No one can dispute that your career matters to you, and you have not said "promote me or I
leave" — but the manager hears it.

**A real competing internal offer, and a manager who keeps stalling** (use once, only with an
offer you would take):
> "The other team's SVP has made me an offer. If I give him my word, I have to keep it — I know
> you'd do the same. I'd really rather stay and do this project with you, but I owe him an
> answer tonight."

It turns pressure on the manager into you keeping a promise, and forces a decision within hours.

**Enlisting instead of demanding:** "How can you help me with X?" makes the other person a
collaborator in solving it, and is harder to refuse than a request.

## General taboos

- Any writing done while you're not calm
- Publicly contradicting your manager in a group chat / meeting
- Over-apologizing (once is enough)
- Stating the other person's motive as if it were fact
