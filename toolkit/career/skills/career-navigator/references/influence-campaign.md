# Influence Campaign — move a decision you have no authority over

A proposal, a cross-team ask, a resource request, getting another team onto your plan: the
people who decide do not report to you, and you still need them to decide. Run it the way an
enterprise sale is run — plan before contact. People prepare hard for an outside customer and
walk into the next team's office with nothing ("we're all on the same side"). When the ask is
territorial, political or zero-sum, the internal stakes are higher, because the loser stays in
the building.

Scale the prep to the stake: a small favour needs one sentence; anything that moves scope,
headcount or a roadmap gets the full sequence below.

## 1. Map the deal

Answer before the first meeting, and record it in the dossier:

- **The decision, the decider, the date.** The highest title is often not the decider — a CTO
  paying two hundred SaaS bills a month decides none of them personally. Find who actually signs
  off, and who has to agree after them.
- **Key influencers.** The platform or data owner, the staff engineer the VP asks, the PM whose
  roadmap you would touch. Title says little; ownership says a lot. Proposals usually die
  before the decision meeting, with an influencer nobody visited. List each one with what they
  gain or lose.
- **Process and calendar.** Planning cycle, budget lock, review meetings. "The budget is spent,
  try next year" and "we just picked another approach" are timing failures, not merit failures.
- **The alternative.** What they do if they say no: nothing, a workaround, another team doing it.
- **Culture.** Top-down (if the exec says no, nothing below moves) or bottom-up (the VP nods,
  then says "now convince my team"); engineering-led or business-led. Organisations are run by
  people, not charts — when a key decider changes, redo the map.

Don't open at the top. A first pitch straight to the most senior person is high-stakes: if it
fails there is no second meeting, and going there before you understand their world annoys
them. Start lower (step 4).

## 2. Answer three questions

Every proposal has to answer all three; missing one stalls it.

| | Check | Failing sign |
|---|---|---|
| **Why buy** | Is the problem worth solving? What does not solving it cost them, in their metric? | They like it but nobody needs it. A few people who need it beat many who like it. |
| **Why you** | Why you or your team, and not another team? What do you have that others don't? | You pitch, your manager agrees, and gives it to another team. Convince yourself first that it has to be you. |
| **Why now** | Which date, planning cycle, launch, incident or competitor makes this quarter the time? | "Sounds great, let's revisit next half." |

Why now is the hardest. A reputation makes people think of you at the right moment, but inbound
does not solve everything — tie the ask to a date they already care about. If you can't answer
one of the three, fix it before the meeting, not in it.

## 3. Pitch from their side

- Lead with what it does for them, in their metric. A technically better thing does not sell
  itself; the classic failure is building something clearly superior and expecting other teams
  to adopt it without saying why it matters to them.
- Ask "so what?" of every sentence. Cut acronyms, internal jargon, concepts that need explaining,
  big words, and technical benefits with no business value.
- The first pitch is not the PhD thesis. Its job is to **generate enough interest** that they ask
  for more: interest in the problem first, then the solution, then the how.
- On a cold start, borrow credibility first — a shared connection, or a result they have heard
  of. Do it at the key meetings, not every one.
- End with the ask: the exact decision or next step.
- If you can't say it simply, you don't understand it well enough yet. That's your problem, not
  the audience's.

> "You mentioned [their goal or pain]. We have a way to cut [metric] by [X] — [one line on why
> us]. It matters now because [date or event]. Could we get 30 minutes with [person] before
> [planning lock] to see whether it fits?"

## 4. Debate prep — don't go in naked

Nobody has the quick wit to invent a strong answer on the spot. People who seem to have it
prepared and stress-tested the objections beforehand.

- For each person on the map, write the objections they could raise and a one-line answer to
  each: a fact, a number, or a relationship (someone they trust who is already on board).
- The worst moment is someone you did not map saying "I have a concern" from an angle you never
  prepared. Before the decision meeting, ask the influencers directly: "What concerns would you
  expect to come up?" — then pre-wire (`office-politics.md`, "Getting inside the circle").
- Climb the levels. Meetings with ICs and managers surface objections cheaply; carry the answers
  up to director, VP, then the decider. Each level up raises the stake.
- Rehearse the two hardest objections with `rehearsal.md`.

When your manager has a high-stakes meeting, offer to do the prep for them: a one-page list of
likely objections with answers, and a view on the format (for a contentious topic, a 1:1 often
beats a crowded room).

## 5. Commitments

Overcommit and underdeliver is the fastest way to lose trust. Inside a company there is no
contract, so expectations drift apart silently. Before committing to a date or scope, use the
ask-before-you-answer script in `tough-conversations.md` ("Asked to commit to a date").

## 6. Negotiate: Open and Walk

Before any negotiation over resources, scope, priority or terms:

- **Open** — the best outcome you can defend. **Walk** — the worst you will accept. The space
  between them is what you negotiate. Back both with facts and data: probing at random marks you
  as unreliable, and that label is expensive.
- Four preparations:
  1. Win-win or zero-sum? Will you need this relationship afterwards?
  2. A convincing reason for your Open and for your Walk.
  3. Align with your manager and stakeholders before you talk to the other side.
  4. Know what you do if it breaks down.
- Gauge the sensitivity, political impact and scope first: who will back you (line them up
  early) and who will oppose (and how you answer them).
- An aggressive, even offensive, first position from the other side is a technique, not a
  character flaw — some companies train it. Don't make it personal. If you decide to walk, be
  ready to own the consequences.
- Count your leverage before you play it (`tough-conversations.md`).

## 7. Orchestrate

What one person can't win, a team can. The people who perform best over years are not lone
heroes; they move resources on both sides.

- **A sponsor** for air cover (`branding-and-sponsorship.md`).
- **Partners** with clear roles: who answers "can it be done", who owns the roadmap promise, who
  handles legal or finance, which executive shows up at the key moment.
- **One metric** that tracks progress, and short regular updates to key stakeholders — no
  surprises.
- **Two levels**: solve this decision (tactical) and why decisions like it keep getting blocked
  (strategic).
- **Ask for help early.** Strong people often miss where their competence ends. "I don't know
  this area" from a senior person is a position of strength.
- **Widen the circle when you hit a wall.** Once everyone in the obvious circle is tried, bring
  in another ring. Most won't help; one may unlock something you never expected.
- **Use your executives.** If a title is needed at the key moment, ask your manager or skip-level
  to appear. Being afraid to ask is not knowing how to use them.

## Output

Give the user:

1. A deal map table: person, role in the decision (decider / influencer / blocker / partner),
   gains or loses, likely objection, next touch and date.
2. The three answers (why buy / why you / why now), one line each, with any gap flagged.
3. The pitch opener, in English.
4. The top three objections with answers.
5. Open and Walk, when a negotiation is involved.
6. The one move this week.
