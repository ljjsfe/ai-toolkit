# Branding, Visibility & Sponsorship

Branding = create positive visibility, become the go-to person. Doing the work isn't enough — you have to talk about it too.

## The six moves of branding

**Speak up · Contribute · Influence · Find opportunities · Keep learning · Practice**

1. **Communication skills**: concise and to the point, understand needs of others, push your agenda, influence directions, get others on the same page
2. **Find opportunities**
   - Proactively ask to present to a bigger audience
   - Proactively ask for high-visibility / critical projects
   - Volunteer to help create visibility for others (helping others = your own branding)
3. **Keep learning**: leadership/management training, leadership events
4. **Practice**: leadership opportunities, volunteer roles, public speaking (know your audience)

## Speak-up tactics

- Before any meeting, prepare **one** substantive point or question, and make sure you say it
- Formula for speaking up: point of view → reasoning → impact on the decision. Don't just raise an issue — bring a proposal
- When someone else has a good idea, credit them publicly ("Building on what X said…") — this also puts you in the discussion
- Don't undersell yourself: when describing your own work, drop "just," "small," "simple"

## Taking the credit you earned

- **Send the first note.** When something ships, be the first to send the congratulations to
  leadership and the team. Even with "together with A and B" in it, the sender gets the largest
  share of the credit.
- **If someone beat you to it, be the colour commentator.** Never stay silent after the PM's
  announcement — reply with the technical half: "Adding to [PM]'s note — in case it's useful for
  leadership, here's a short write-up of how we solved the scaling problem underneath." The
  announcer calls the play; the colour commentator explains why it worked. Nobody objects to
  that.
- **Name it at its biggest true size.** Pick the shortest, strongest label that is true under a
  fair reading: "the first engineer on Prime Video" rather than "an engineer on Amazon's first
  video product", even if it wasn't called Prime yet. The test: it survives anyone who was there
  checking it. Past that line it is a lie, and one exposed lie costs every label after it.

## Opportunities to share your work (goal checklist for the first 6 months in a new environment)

- [ ] One tech talk within the team (a recent project, a design decision, a lesson learned)
- [ ] One cross-team share-out or brown bag
- [ ] One internal doc that gets cited by another team
- [ ] One presentation to senior leadership or a key stakeholder (with your manager present or aware)
- [ ] One external activity: meetup / LinkedIn article / industry event

## Sponsorship

**To advance, you need good sponsors to give feedback.** A mentor gives you advice; a sponsor speaks for you in rooms you're not in.

**Direct manager (your first sponsor)**
- 1:1s are non-negotiable
- Ask for performance feedback every 6-12 weeks
- Ask directly: What do I need to achieve to get promoted?
- Know their deliverables and obstacles, and help solve them

**Other team leaders**
- Set up a 1:1 every 3 months: connect, ask for feedback & advice
- Opener: "I'm new to the team — I'd love 30 min to learn how your team works with ours and what you've seen work well."
- These are the people who can be your +1 in the calibration room

**What qualifies a sponsor — all three, or they can't help**
1. **A seat at the table**: they are in the closed-door meetings where promotions, ratings and
   scope are decided.
2. **They know your work specifically**: when they speak for you, they can say what you did, not
   "she's great".
3. **Real clout**: their word moves the room. A seat with no weight is a wasted vote.
Values and fit decide *which* qualified person to approach, never whether an unqualified one will do.

**Getting close without being heavy**
Skip the dinners, gifts and résumés — too heavy, and it scares people off. A relationship is
contact frequency: grab a coffee and ask one question, ask about Friday's weekend plans and
Monday how it went, bring a specific problem and ask for their read. Three months of that, and
you are a different species from the colleague with their head down.

**Ask directly — after the work is visible**
> "You know me and you know my work. I'd like you to sponsor me."

The worst case is a no — and then they owe you the reason, which is worth more than the yes:
it is the gap list from someone in the room. Ask in the year that matters, and only once your
record clears the leverage inventory in `tough-conversations.md`; asking before the work is
visible spends the relationship for nothing.

**Sponsor map (update every quarter)**
```
Name | Role | Which room they have a voice in | What they know about my work | What I've helped them with | Next touchpoint
```

## Improving yourself (the prerequisite for a sponsor to invest in you)

- Practise the one presentation skill that is costing you: record your next update and fix the worst habit (filler words, rushing, burying the ask)
- **Earn respect** — domain knowledge, execution, leadership. Results earn respect
- **Do what you said, every time** — one missed commitment costs more trust than three delivered ones earn; meet people when you don't need anything yet, so the first ask isn't the first contact
- Find a good mentor — someone to help you navigate a complex environment (one outside the company, and one inside who's two levels above you)

## Designing how you are perceived

Perception is built on purpose, not left to happen. When a senior leader told Carla Harris she
wasn't "tough" enough, she ran a 90-day plan and wired toughness into every behaviour until the
label stuck.

1. Pick **three adjectives** you want people to use about you. Each must be true of you and
   valued for the role you want (e.g. decisive, technically deep, calm under pressure).
2. For 90 days, make every visible behaviour prove them: how you walk into the meeting, what you
   say first, every email, how you react when something breaks.
3. Consistency is the method. Doing it some days teaches people nothing.
4. It is an amplifier, not a photocopier: it makes visible a trait you already have. A false
   persona falls apart under pressure and takes your credibility with it.

## Executive presence

Roughly: 60% gravitas (your record — what you've delivered and survived), 30% communication, 10%
appearance. So the fastest lever is the record; the delivery only amplifies it.

- Lower pitch, slower pace, a deliberate pause after the key sentence. Calm under pressure — the
  room reads panic before it reads content.
- State conclusions, not hedges: "We should do B" rather than "I think maybe B could work."
- Use a specific number you can source: "in 3 of the last 4 launches", "this cut p99 by 40%".
  Specificity raises confidence; a made-up figure ("studies show 62%...") collapses at the first
  "source?" and takes your credibility with it.

## Fitting in (a common gap for engineers whose home workplace culture differs from US norms, for example many immigrant engineers)

- Be willing to take more risks: take on risky projects, fail fast, learn fast
- Don't avoid confrontation: push back on unreasonable requests to earn respect; don't be too nice
- Speak up with substance
- Give credit when credit is due (different from kissing up)
- Fit in with the local culture; support and help each other grow
- Learn the local unwritten rules from someone who has succeeded there: ask a senior peer "what do people here get wrong in their first year?"
