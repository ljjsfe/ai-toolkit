# Strategic Decision（战略决策）

Two modes. **Quick** for most decisions; **full** when the decision is hard to reverse or the
stakes are large. Name the mode at the start.

## Iron laws

1. **Baseline and base rate before analysis.** One sentence: the plain reading. Then the
   outside view: what usually happens in cases like this (the reference class), with a rough
   rate. Inside-view details adjust from there. Overturning the baseline needs named evidence. A common form of such evidence: a structural
   shift the strong incumbent is unable to follow (new cost structure, new channel, new
   technology).
2. **Hard floors filter options.** List what earlier commitments already decide (status,
   contracts, money, family, health). An option that breaks a floor is removed, whatever its
   upside.
3. **Accuracy over balance.** Delete a "but" that your own analysis has refuted; do not add
   qualifiers for the appearance of nuance. Keep genuine tail risks — low probability, high
   impact — as named risks with a trigger, not as hedges in the conclusion.
4. **Calibrated ranges, not adjectives.** Every key judgement gets a probability range. Would
   you bet real money at it? If not, the range is too narrow. A range wider than 40 points
   means information is missing — go get it (below) instead of analysing further. If no
   affordable information would narrow it, decide on reversibility (law 5).
5. **Stop when analysis stops yielding** — when another round would not change the answer,
   when every surviving option is cheap to reverse, or when waiting has become the costliest
   choice. Cycling the same options a third time is avoidance.

## Quick mode

1. Baseline + base rate (law 1), and the order-and-institution checks in this skill's SKILL.md that apply.
2. Hard floors and the options that survive them.
3. For each option: reversibility, what you learn by trying it, what it rules out, payoff timing.
4. Two or three key judgements with probability ranges.
5. Decision, the earliest signal that it is working, and the date to revisit.

## Full mode — adds these after step 3, before the probability ranges of step 4

**Stakeholders.** Who benefits, who is threatened, who controls the resource you need, who is
indifferent but could turn; formal authority versus real influence; what sponsors gain by
helping. Then the reaction check: will your move change their strategy? If so, re-run their
interests with the new strategy.

**Second-order effects, pressure-tested.** For each "because Y, then Z", ask:
1. Does Z depend on someone leaving or retaliating — and do they have a real alternative?
2. If Z happens, does it change the structure of the situation, or only add friction?
3. Does the leverage involved need to last forever, or only for a finite window?
4. Is the cost of maintaining the current state as high as assumed?
Downgrade Z to "friction" only when the answers show it cannot change the outcome. Do not
delete it for failing one question; record why it was downgraded.

**Threat grading.** A: can change the outcome (a competitor with a structural cost advantage, a
regulation that removes the market, the loss of a key person). B: annoying but cannot change
the outcome (noise competitors, minor process friction). Do not escalate B to strategy.

**Opposing framings — to find the crux.** Run the analysis under the two most plausible
opposing framings. If they agree, confidence rises only as far as they rest on *different*
evidence; shared premises can make both wrong together. If they disagree, the point of
disagreement is the real decision variable — go and resolve that.

**Pre-mortem.** Assume it failed in 12 months: the three likeliest causes, the earliest warning
sign of each, what you would do on seeing it at month 3, and the one assumption whose failure
cascades.

**Information-gap triage.** For each wide range: more thinking (concept unclear) or new
information (facts, others' judgement, reactions)? For the latter, the cheapest action first:
search (minutes) → ask someone (hours) → send a signal and watch (days) → small experiment
(weeks).

**Compare options with reasons, not invented scores.** Rank them with the decisive reasons;
weight reversible options favourably — an irreversible choice needs a clearly higher expected
value.

## Depth illusion and bias check

Finding a counter-intuitive risk is not the goal; being right is. Pre-mortems and
"every upside hides a downside" tilt pessimistic, while deleting hedges tilts optimistic.
After the analysis, compare it with the baseline and base rate: a large departure needs
evidence proportional to the departure.

## Geopolitical and power situations

- **Then what?** Trace from an action to the end state the actor wants, and find where the
  chain breaks.
- **Leverage shelf life:** must this leverage hold permanently, or only long enough to build
  an alternative? Most leverage is transitional.
- **Window convergence (why now):** see `cross-domain-synthesis.md`; in power situations raise
  the probability of action only on observable preparation.
- Keep contested political positions out of the analysis; describe actors' stated aims and
  observable capabilities.

## Career and long-runway positioning

- **Structural arbitrage** (see `cross-domain-synthesis.md`): choose the system, not only the
  effort.
- **Compounding moves:** prefer actions whose output feeds the next one (a case study → a
  public write-up → an invitation → a wider network).
- **The "enough" check:** am I doing this to prove myself (no stopping point) or to use myself
  (self-sustaining)?

## Output

```
Mode:              quick / full
Baseline:          one sentence · base rate for similar cases
Floors:            hard constraints · options removed by them
Options:           with reversibility and timing
Key judgements:    each with a probability range
Decision:          the choice · earliest success signal · revisit date
(Full mode adds)   stakeholders · tested second-order effects · crux · pre-mortem · information actions
```
