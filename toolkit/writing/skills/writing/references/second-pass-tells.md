# Second-pass tells and the writer behind the draft

Read after the step-6 structural pass when the draft is clean but still reads as generated,
or when there is no `PERSONAL.md` and the draft sounds like nobody. A voice profile overrides
everything here (Iron law 3): a habit the profile shows the writer uses on purpose stays.

## Second-generation tells

These appear once the stock words and false contrasts are gone: the model has swapped one set
of habits for another. "Remove" means delete unless the voice profile shows the writer does
it; "limit" means at most one per piece, and only when it says something true.

| tell | looks like | fix |
|---|---|---|
| sentences shaped like wisdom (remove) | "Good tools disappear." "Clarity is kindness." | a line nobody would dispute; replace it with the fact it points at, or cut it |
| performed cleverness (limit) | "Your backlog, minus the guilt." | a witty pairing built for effect; say the plain thing unless the line is also true and in the writer's voice |
| staccato bursts (limit) | "It's fast. It's cheap. It works." then one long sentence | a rhythm device; merge into one sentence that carries a fact |
| perfect balance (limit) | every section the same length, every point the same weight | let the section the writer knows best run long and shrink or drop the weakest |
| omniscient casual (limit) | a relaxed voice that is certain about everything | scope each claim; say what the writer does not know and who this is not for |
| self-narration (remove) | "Here's why this matters." "Now it gets interesting." | lines that talk about the text; delete, and add a detail if the point needs help |
| tidy closers (remove) | "Because your readers deserve better." | a quotable last line that fits any piece; end on the number, the case, the judgement or the ask |
| reflexive specificity (limit) | a round statistic or a neat anecdote on every claim | specificity used as a style; every number and story must come from a source or the writer, otherwise `[NEED: proof]` |

Check: read the last line of every section for a tidy closer, look for sentences shaped like
wisdom, and compare section lengths — if they are all about the same, the draft is too balanced.

## The writer behind the draft

Removing tells leaves prose that is clean and flat. Readable prose comes from a particular
person with uneven knowledge and real opinions. Where no voice profile exists, ask the writer
(in the step-2 batch of first-hand questions) rather than answering for them:

1. Which parts do you know cold? Those get the most words and the most detail.
2. Which parts bore you or sit outside what you know? Those get compressed or cut.
3. What is your arguable opinion about the problem or the usual way of handling it?
4. If a third had to go, what goes first? Cut it now.

What the answers produce:

- **Uneven density** — the part the writer cares about runs longest; equal coverage of
  everything is a tell.
- **Honest limits** — where the writer does not know, the piece skips it or says so.
- **Opinions that cost something** — specific enough that someone could disagree.
- **Sentence length follows the thought** — not a planned short-short-long rhythm.
- **First person only where it carries a fact** — "I rebuilt this after the outage" earns
  the "I"; first person that adds no fact is decoration.

**Never invent experience.** The writer's stories, results, customers and quotes come from
the writer or a source (Iron laws 1–2); a missing one is `[NEED: what is missing]`. The
upstream lets a model construct a persona's opinions and emphasis; this skill does not —
opinions are first-hand too, so a missing one is asked for or marked `[NEED: your view on …]`
(toolkit policy). With a voice profile in place, match the profile; do not build a new
persona on top of it.
